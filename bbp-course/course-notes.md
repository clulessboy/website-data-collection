## Purpose of this course
- The purpose of this course is to give you basic training on the hazards of bloodborne pathogens to meet the training requirements of the L & I Bloodborne Pathogens Rule. Your employer must meet the requirements of that rule if there is a possibility you could be exposed to blood or body fluids at your job. This training is generic and your employer must also train you on the specific bloodborne pathogen hazards that exist where you work and how they will protect you from those hazards.

## What are bloodborne pathogens?
- HIV infected T-cell
- Bloodborne pathogens are viruses that can be present in blood or in "Other Potentially Infectious Material" (OPIM) and cause serious diseases. HIV and Hepatitis B and C are the main viruses of concern in the workplace.
- Other bloodborne pathogens

## OPIM includes the following:
- - Semen, - Vaginal secretions - Pleural, cerebrospinal, pericardial, peritoneal, synovial, and amniotic body fluids, - Saliva with blood in dental procedures - Any body fluids visibly contaminated with blood
- - Unknown body fluids - Any unfixed tissue or organ (other than intact skin) from a human (living or dead) - HIV, HCV, or HBV-containing cultures, culture medium, or other solutions - Blood, organs, and tissues from humans infected with HIV, HCV, HBV, or other BBPs

## Bloodborne pathogens can enter your body by:
- contaminated instrument injuries, a break in the skin (cut, lesion), mucus membranes (eyes, nose, mouth), sexual contact injection drug use (shared needles)
- hand holding scalpel

## Hepatitis and the liver
- liver in a human body
- The liver is a vital organ of your body that aids digestion, filters blood and fights infections. Toxins, certain drugs, heavy alcohol use, some diseases, and certain bacterial and viral infections can all cause hepatitis. The hepatitis viruses infect the liver, causing inflammation, enlargement and tenderness.

## Hepatitis B Virus - HBV

## Difference between chronic and acute hepatitis B
- Acute Hepatitis B Infection
- May last up to six months (with or without symptoms) Infected people can infect others by sharing needles. Most do not experience symptoms, but some can experience severe ones. The immune system can clear the infection.
- Chronic Hepatitis B Infection
- Long-term illness. Occurs when the hepatitis B virus remains in a person’s body for more than six months (after first blood test result). Most people don’t show any symptoms. The immune system doesn’t clear the infection.

## Hepatitis C virus (HCV)
- The most common bloodborne infection in the U.S. with 3.2 million being infected. 12,000 deaths from HCV infections each year. Ranges in severity from a mild illness, lasting a few weeks, to a serious lifelong illness. It can be either “acute” or “chronic”. Drugs are now available to treat infected people.
- progression of infection

## Hepatitis C symptoms
- Flu-like symptoms: Jaundice Fatigue Joint Pain Dark urine Abdominal pain Loss of appetite Nausea Long-term effects
- person with jaundice

## Difference between acute and chronic hepatitis C
- Acute hepatitis C infection
- Short term illness. Occurs within the first six months after exposure. For most people, acute infection will lead to chronic infection if no treatment.
- Chronic hepatitis C infection
- Long-term illness. Occurs when the hepatitis C virus remains in the body. It can last a lifetime without treatment. It can lead to serious liver problems, including cirrhosis (scarring of the liver) or liver cancer.

## HIV
- Fragile—survives only a few hours in dry environment Attacks the human immune system One million+ infected in U.S. Cause of AIDS if untreated No vaccine yet, but drug treatments have prolonged life of infected people
- HIV Medication
- HIV medication

## Untreated HIV infection can lead to AIDS
- Most people infected with HIV will develop AIDS within 10-12 years if not treated early with antiretroviral drugs. AIDS-related symptoms include TB, skin tumors, weight loss, mental impairment and many others. Death occurs within 3 years.
- progression of AIDS of no treatment

## Immediate medical treatment if exposed to HIV
- If you are exposed to HIV, start the PEP (Post-Exposure Prophylaxis) treatment within 72 hours after exposure. PEP can reduce your chance of getting HIV infection. The sooner the exposed person starts PEP, the better. Every hour counts!

## Two definitions
- Occupational Exposure” means reasonably anticipated skin, eye, mucous membrane, or piercing of the skin, contact with blood or OPIM that may result from the performance of an employee's duties.
- needle stick on finger
- “Exposure Incident” means an actual eye, mouth, other mucous membrane, non-intact skin or skin piercing contact with blood or OPIM while performing your work duties.

## Bloodborne pathogen exposure controls
- Universal precautions (or standard precautions) Equipment and safer medical devices Safe work practices Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) Laundry handling Handling BBP waste materials

## What are Universal Precautions/Standard Precautions?
- Standard Precautions combine the major features of Universal Precautions and Body Substance Isolation and are based on the principle that all blood, body fluids, secretions, and excretions except sweat, non-intact skin, and mucous membranes may contain transmissible infectious agents. Standard Precautions include a group of infection prevention practices that apply to all patients, regardless of suspected or confirmed infection status, in any setting in which healthcare is delivered.

## Handling needles safely
- Don’t bend, recap, or remove needles or other sharps. Don’t shear or break needles. Place contaminated reusable sharps immediately in appropriate containers until properly decontaminated.

## Safe work practices
- Don’t eat, drink, smoke, apply cosmetics or lip balm, or handle contact lenses in any work areas where there is the possibility of exposure to blood or OPIM. Don’t place food or drink in refrigerators, freezers, shelves, cabinets, countertops or bench tops in any other work areas where blood or OPIM is located.

## Cleaning contaminated surfaces
- All work surfaces and equipment contaminated with blood or OPIM must be cleaned up with an appropriate disinfectant as soon as possible or according to your employer's written schedule.
- person using a disinfectant

## Clean-up of spills and broken glassware/sharps
- Use paper/absorbent towels to soak up the spilled materials. Clean the area with 10% bleach or EPA-registered disinfectant. Saturate the spill area with disinfectant. Leave for 10 minutes (or as specified by product manufacturer) or allow to air dry. Properly dispose of paper towels and cleaning materials into proper waste containers.

- Lab coat Gown Apron Surgical cap or hood Shoe cover or boot

## Laundry contaminated with blood or OPIM
- Handle as little as possible. Bag/containerize at point of use. Don’t sort or rinse at point of use. Place wet laundry in leak-proof, labeled or color-coded containers/bags.

## Handling BBP waste containers
- Close immediately before removing or replacing. Place in second container if leaking possible or if outside contamination of primary container occurs. If reusable: open, empty and clean it in a manner that will not expose you and other employees.
- person holding a red waste bag

## Biohazard labels and signs
- Containers with blood or OPIM must have the biohazard symbol. Labels attached securely to any containers or items containing blood/OPIM. Red bags/containers may substitute for labels. Signs posted at entrance to specified work areas.
- biohazard warning sign
- another biohazard warning sign

## What to do if you are exposed to blood or OPIM
- Wash needlesticks, cuts, and skin with soap and water. Flush splashes to the nose and mouth with water. Irrigate eyes with clean water, saline, or a sterile irrigant. Report exposure to your supervisor. Schedule visit to doctor as soon as possible.
- a needle stick accident

## Actual contact: What your employer must do for you
- Offer Hepatitis B vaccine, Pay for your medical costs if you are exposed, Keep confidential medical records, Provide a post-exposure medical exam.

## Hepatitis B vaccine for exposed workers
- Provided at no cost to you Three shots at 0, 1, & 6 months Effective for 95% of adults Also protects after exposure

- These records are confidential and include: Hepatitis B vaccination and post-exposure evaluations Health care provider’s written opinions Information provided to healthcare provider as required Must be maintained for length of employment + 30 years.
